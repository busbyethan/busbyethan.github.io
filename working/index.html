A central research program at SAIL · Work in progress

Learning to disagree, productively.

Can AI help people build the skills to navigate political disagreement—and use those skills in everyday life?

Our team is developing a research program around a simple premise: handling disagreement can be learned. We are studying how to assess people's strengths, offer focused training, and provide practice conversations with AI partners.

We focus on five abilities: participating in difficult conversations, expressing a view, giving reasons, listening, and reconsidering one's own perspective. The goal is to help people become more capable conversation partners beyond the training itself.

Assess skillsLearn & practiceApply beyond the app

The training system and its effects are being developed and evaluated. Transfer to real conversations is a research goal, rather than an established result.

With Lisa P. Argyle, Joshua R. Gubler, Alex Lyman, and David Wingate.

Work in progress · Political communication

What does trying to persuade do to the speaker?

Political conversation can change the person doing the talking as well as the person listening. With Christopher F. Karpowitz and Lisa P. Argyle, I am examining how the topic and purpose of speech shape speakers' experiences and attitudes.

Participants record videos about political or nonpolitical topics, with instructions to persuade or offer a balanced account. We examine what they say, how they speak, and how they feel afterward. This project asks what kinds of speaking experiences leave people more open to other perspectives.

Working manuscript: Trying to Change Your Mind Changed Me: The Impact of Political Speech on the Speaker.

Work in progress · AI & causal inference

Can AI simulate an experiment—or just a correlation?

When a language model produces responses that look human, does it also reproduce what an experiment would tell us? We are investigating how well AI-generated data distinguish causal effects from observational associations.

We compare simulations with human experimental data and test controlled scenarios, including the difference between assignment to a treatment and actually receiving it. The project examines how results depend on prompting, model choices, and the groups being represented. Our aim is to clarify when these tools can support credible inference and where they fall short.

Early-stage manuscript: Experimental and Observational Inferences from LLMs. With Lisa P. Argyle, Joshua R. Gubler, Alex Lyman, Bryce Hepner, and David Wingate. Analyses and framing are still in development.
